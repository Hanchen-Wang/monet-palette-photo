# Source-responsive palette engine

Use this reference for the Monet zones selected by the card. The photograph remains the factual color source; the engine reorganizes source evidence into impressionist color relationships.

## Color decomposition

Do not reduce a region to its object label or one local color. Build each principal Monet zone from five to nine color families selected from these roles:

1. dominant local family;
2. light-bearing family;
3. cool counter-family;
4. warm counter-family;
5. reflected or environmental family;
6. chromatic dark family;
7. optional transition family;
8. optional high-chroma spark;
9. optional atmospheric veil.

Not every role needs a separate hue. Merge roles when the source is quiet; never add color merely to fill the quota.

For `sparse_social`, five to nine roles may still be recorded in the card, but only three to six need to become visibly active families. Consolidate related roles and reserve the clearest warm/cool or light/dark event for the thumbnail anchor. Quiet fields should use one restrained family or a close interval rather than small marks from every family.

## Chromatic darks

Dark zones should remain chromatic. Prefer a source-responsive mixture such as blue-violet, deep cobalt, muted green, mauve, or a warm/cool complementary mixture over neutral black. Use near-black only when a protected source element genuinely requires it, and keep neighboring dark notes colored.

## Light-temperature rules

### Warm light

- highlights may move toward cream, yellow, peach, or warm rose;
- shadows counter with blue, violet, or cool green;
- retain source-specific material color beneath the temperature shift.

### Cool or overcast light

- highlights may move toward cool white, gray-blue, or pale lavender;
- shadows may use mauve, blue-green, or soft violet;
- keep contrast and saturation consistent with observed diffusion.

### Sunset or strongly warm conditions

- warm families may include orange, rose, or gold when observed;
- counterbalance with lavender, blue, or violet;
- do not invent a sunset when the source does not show one.

## Water and reflective surfaces

Water is not automatically blue. Construct it from:

- observed sky color;
- surrounding environment color;
- reflected subject color;
- the surface's own local hue;
- light and chromatic dark families.

Keep reflection color aligned to the real subject and axis. Glass, wet pavement, and metal follow the same relational rule with material-specific brush behavior.

## Warm–cool vibration

Place warm and cool notes near one another when the source supports optical variation. Do not blend every passage into a smooth gradient. Preserve broken adjacency at M2–M4, while keeping the underlying value structure readable.

## Strength mapping

- **M1:** two or three quiet temperature shifts inside the source palette.
- **M2:** visible warm/cool adjacency and one chromatic-dark family.
- **M3:** full five-to-nine-family decomposition with clear optical mixing.
- **M4:** broader nonliteral color families are allowed, but they must remain traceable to source light, environment, or semantic emphasis.

## Hard avoids

- “pastel” as a universal shortcut;
- grass equals one green, sky equals one blue, shadow equals gray;
- arbitrary rainbow palettes;
- fluorescent digital color unrelated to the source;
- identical palette behavior across water, skin, foliage, architecture, and atmosphere.
